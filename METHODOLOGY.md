# Methodology

This is how I turn an AI idea into something a salesperson could use.

## 1. Start With the Job

Start with a sales task that comes up again and again and is slow, patchy or easy to miss. Don't start with a model, or a new feature looking for a problem.

## 2. Use Only What Is Needed

Gather the least approved information the task needs. Check it's current, and take out personal or confidential details that don't belong in the workflow.

## 3. Decide Which Sources Matter Most

I usually trust sources in this order:

1. Direct statements from the customer
2. Agreed actions, dates and commitments
3. Current CRM records, including stage history, not just the current stage
4. Current email
5. Meeting notes or call transcripts
6. Public information
7. AI interpretation

Meeting notes and email don't replace the CRM, and the CRM doesn't replace them. Each can hold something the others don't. A CRM's stage history can show a clear decline or a corrected stage that a meeting summary may miss. An email thread can hold the customer's exact words, which a CRM field rarely does. When a deal lives in more than one of these systems, check them all before you decide what state it's in. If you work only from the system that's easiest to search, you can reach a wrong conclusion and feel sure of it.

If two sources disagree, the output should show that. It shouldn't quietly pick the handiest version.

## 4. Weigh Every Kind of Source the Same Way

Section 3 covers which evidence to trust most about a specific deal. But not every claim a workflow makes is about one deal. A workflow might cite a named sales method, what a product is documented to do or a piece of public research. Those need their own order of trust:

1. Direct evidence supplied for the task, which is what section 3 covers
2. Official product or platform documentation: what the product is documented to do, not what someone assumes or hopes it does
3. Public primary research: a named study, survey or dataset, not someone else's summary of one
4. Named sales methods, such as SPIN, MEDDIC or Challenger, cited by name so a reader can check the source, not borrowed quietly as if they were original
5. Material from a practitioner you can identify: a named person's published view, not an unsourced "best practice"
6. The model's interpretation, labelled as such, never passed off as one of the five sources above

This order is there for a few reasons.

A vendor's claim about its product isn't the same as a customer's confirmed need. Product documentation can confirm what a tool does. It can't confirm that a particular prospect needs it. Keep those two questions apart, even when one paragraph touches both.

Public company information is background, not evidence of an internal problem. A hiring page or press release can shape a guess. It can't stand in for something the prospect confirmed.

An estimate stays an estimate, wherever it came from. A number nobody measured, whether it came from the customer, a practitioner or public research, is an estimate, not a fact. Label it as one under the rule below.

If two sources at different levels disagree, say so. Don't quietly settle it in favour of the higher one. If a named sales method points one way and the customer's own stated preference points the other, that's a real disagreement. Name it; don't smooth it over.

## 5. Separate Facts From Judgement

Label important information as one of these:

- Confirmed: the source material says it directly
- Estimate: a number or view given without measuring it
- Inference: a reasonable reading that needs checking
- Unknown: missing, unclear or contradictory

## 6. Let AI Prepare the Work

AI can sort information, find gaps, suggest questions and write first drafts. It isn't the source of truth, and it doesn't make the commercial decision.

## 7. Keep a Person Responsible

A salesperson checks the output, decides what matters and approves anything that leaves the company or changes a system of record.

## 8. Score the Result

A polished answer can still be wrong. Test each workflow for accuracy, usefulness, tone, privacy and invented claims.

## 9. Record What Went Wrong

For every test, note what the AI missed, overstated or got wrong. Then decide whether the fix belongs in the prompt, the workflow or the checking.

## 10. Keep It Portable

The sales method underneath should work in any AI product. You can add tool-specific instructions later without rebuilding the whole workflow.
