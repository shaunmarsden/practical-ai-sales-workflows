# Evidence Status

This page separates what's available from what I've tested.

A workflow, skill or polished example is useful, but it doesn't prove another salesperson can use it. The last column changes only when someone outside this project runs a test and I log what they found.

Every evaluation linked below names what went wrong in the run it scored. [Which AI Mistakes Actually Get Through](guides/which-ai-mistakes-get-through.md) collects many of those faults in one place and sorts them by whether a careful reader would have noticed. That's a different question from how a run scored.

## How to Read the Matrix

- **Workflow** means there's a plain-English method.
- **Skill** means there's a reusable AI instruction for that sales job.
- **Fictional test** means there's a public worked case or scored evaluation using fictional information.
- **Real use** means I've used the method on real sales work, with private details removed. I never publish real customer or employer material.
- **External user test** means someone outside this project has tried it on their own. Nobody has, for any job. The smallest useful version is one person scoring one output against the rubric, which takes about 15 minutes: [score this yourself](evaluations/score-this-yourself.md).

## Evidence That Is Not Per Job

The matrix covers the seventeen sales jobs. Two things I tested sit outside it, and you'd miss both if you took the table as the whole picture.

- [Cross-model post-call comparison](evaluations/cross-model-post-call-comparison.md). I ran the same Hartwell transcript and post-call prompt through several AI tools on 19 July 2026. I compared them on how many corrections each needed, not on which answer read best. Along with the ambiguous objection test below, it's the only evidence here on whether a method holds up when the tool changes.
- The sales copilot method, which chains several skills instead of running one. It has [a scored fictional review](evaluations/fictional-sales-copilot-review.md) against the same rubric, [a finding from my own internal use](evaluations/sales-copilot-internal-use-finding.md) and [a real-use finding](evaluations/sales-copilot-live-run-finding.md) covering one real request. The [roadmap](ROADMAP.md) names a test by someone else as its gap.

I ran both myself, like everything else on this page.

## Tests With More Than One Run

There are 18. Two ran the same input several times to see how much a score moves with nothing changed. Fourteen compared an instruction with and without one change. One compared two versions of a scenario rather than of an instruction. One compared a method I'd drafted with the plain instruction it would have replaced. The [stacked figure test](evaluations/business-case-stacked-figure-test.md) reused three runs from the [check requirement test](evaluations/business-case-check-requirement-test.md) for its plain baseline instead of running its own. I scored 12 of the tests without knowing which version produced each run: the applied examples test and every later one except the CRM hygiene prompt test.

For a long time this page didn't link the first two. That's how I came to claim in the [comparison](COMPARISON.md) that no test had ever been repeated. It had.

- [The ambiguous objection stability test](evaluations/hartwell-objection-ambiguous-test.md) is the big one: one input, nine runs, three each on three models. Claude scored 46 to 49 and picked a different main diagnosis in each of its three runs. ChatGPT scored 47 three times and Gemini 45, 45 and 46, and both kept the same diagnosis. Claude peaked higher but was less consistent.
- [Three single repeats](evaluations/repeat-run-findings.md) of the chase decision, CRM hygiene review and post-call evidence tests. One held exactly and two came out higher.
- [The business case stacked figure test](evaluations/business-case-stacked-figure-test.md) made 19 runs across a two by two grid. It tested a guardrail against multiplying two unmeasured figures together. The test supported it and I kept it: none of the ten runs with it produced such a figure, against four of the nine without. A six-run follow-up checked it wasn't too blunt, and all six gave the confirmed arithmetic that the two measured scenarios support. It's the first change here a test has supported. The page also records a mistake I made partway through, when I read too much into three runs without the change.
- [The business case check requirement test](evaluations/business-case-check-requirement-test.md) made six runs of one scenario, three on the skill as published and three with one line added. It tested a claim I'd already published about why a prompt outscored the skill, and the claim failed. It also found that four of the six runs multiplied two unmeasured figures into a single pound total, one of them a yearly total of £131,000. The published skill gave the worst run of the six.
- [The applied examples test](evaluations/business-case-applied-examples-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether the business case skill drops its worked example. Both versions gave one every time, so I didn't adopt the change. Checking the wording first showed that half the premise was my own reporting error: only the prompt asks for three examples, and the six earlier runs behind that claim were all runs of the skill. It's the first test here I scored without knowing which version produced each run.
- [The stacked figure guardrail on the prompt](evaluations/business-case-prompt-guardrail-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, adding to the prompt the guardrail the skill already had. None of six against five of six, one-tailed p of 0.0076, so I adopted it. **It also measures a cost the skill's own test only hinted at.** No run with the guardrail gave any overall sense of scale, not even the hours total the criterion allows, against four of six without it.
- [The business case padding test](evaluations/business-case-padding-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether the prompt's firm "Three applied examples" pushes a run into inventing two. It doesn't. Neither version gave an example with nothing behind it, and all 12 gave one and said why there weren't three. **It found a worse fault while looking.** Nine of the 12 produced the stacked figure, because the guardrail against it is in the skill, not the prompt, and the recipe card carries the prompt.
- [Should the ledger be printed](evaluations/chase-ledger-printing-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version. The instruction called the ledger a working step, but five of six runs printed it anyway in each of two earlier tests. The test asks whether the instruction can be made to hold before asking whether printing it is good, because an instruction the model ignores is wrong either way. Saying so plainly stops it, zero of six against six of six, and every run in both versions still stated the date clash. It also puts a number on a trade-off nobody had measured: the ledger is about 139 words, roughly a quarter of the output.
- [The ledger step where nothing collides](evaluations/chase-no-collision-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether a step that requires a collision check invents a collision when there's nothing to find. Neither version produced a false one, so I kept the step. It needed a new scenario, since no published input here had dates and an absence that didn't overlap. It closes the gap for false positives only.
- [The ledger step on the skill](evaluations/chase-skill-ledger-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, taking the step that worked on the chase prompt and testing it on the skill. Six of six against zero of six, p of 0.0011, so I adopted it there too. It also records a prediction I wrote down beforehand and got wrong: the published skill was worse at this than the published prompt, not better. The earlier record that suggested otherwise had used a looser reading.
- [The dated commitment ledger test](evaluations/chase-dated-commitment-ledger-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether a required step does better than a principle at getting the chase prompt to state a date clash it kept missing. Five of six against two of six, so I adopted the step. The page says plainly that a one-tailed p of 0.12 falls short of the level I treat as a result elsewhere here. It's the third test on that one fault and, after 36 runs, the first change it hasn't rejected.
- [The chase weekday re-run](evaluations/chase-weekday-rerun.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether naming the weekdays in the chase scenario produced the behaviour its answer key asks for. It didn't: one of six against none of six, below the threshold I set beforehand. The change stays anyway, because it fixed an over-claim, not a behaviour. The instruction and the input are both ruled out as the cause, and I haven't found what is.
- [The chase stale-date test](evaluations/chase-stale-date-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether the chase prompt's wording on stale dates was too narrow to cover a commitment the prospect made themselves. Four of six runs of the published prompt named it. That met the condition I'd set for rejecting the idea, and puts the original miss within the prompt's normal variation, so I didn't adopt the change. It also found that the scenario's answer key claims a date conflict the input never states, which three runs spotted.
- [The em dash rule test](evaluations/em-dash-rule-test.md) made 33 runs on the least interesting fault here. All 28 runs without the rule produced em dashes; the five with it produced none. It fixes upkeep and disclosure, not output quality. I tested one skill and changed 13, and the page is clear that this stretches one result to cover the rest.
- [The proof point selection test](evaluations/proof-point-selection-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether a proof-point method I'd drafted beat the instruction a seller would type. The two were the same on all seven failure conditions and all three positive criteria I'd decided in advance. I'd also decided not to publish the method if the plain instruction had no failures, and it had none. It's the only test here of a whole new method, and I didn't add it.
- [The CRM hygiene prompt missing-record test](evaluations/crm-hygiene-prompt-missing-record-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether the step already added to the skill also works in the pasteable prompt. It does. None of six filed a record that doesn't exist as an ordinary missing field, against six of six before, and six of six named the outside evidence to check, against none before. One run of the published prompt went further than any of the skill's six runs and told the user to create the record. The page also records a scoring mistake I caught by reading the runs rather than searching them for patterns.
- [The business case second line test](evaluations/business-case-prompt-second-line-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether one more line could bring back the hours total the stacked-figure guardrail had suppressed. It did, in five of six against none of six in a control run the same day. The pound figure stayed at none in both versions, as did any hours-saved figure built from the untested reduction. One-tailed p of 0.0076.
- [The business case skill second line test](evaluations/business-case-skill-second-line-test.md) made 12 runs, six per version, on whether the skill needed the same line as the prompt. The control answered that first: the published skill gave the hours total in none of six, suppressing it just as the prompt had. With the line, six of six, and the pound figure stayed at none in both. One-tailed p of 0.0011. The page records that the skill's own footer describes the fault, which weakens the pound-figure zero. It also records a search that failed silently and would have reversed the result.

Every other scored test on this page is a single run. Where a job above shows two, three or four scored cases, those are separate scenarios, not repeats of one.

Read the first two together. Claude's spread on this rubric is about three points, so a three-point rise on a repeat is within the range the nine-run test already found. The more interesting result there is that a steady total hid an unsteady judgement.

The 16 comparisons are worth reading for a different reason. Nine supported the change they tested, one of them only weakly. One tested an adopted change for harm rather than benefit, and found none. Six didn't support the change they tested: one rejected the idea and turned up a live fault that had nothing to do with it, three ended with the change not adopted, one didn't produce the behaviour it was after, and the proof point method added nothing. One of the nine fixed an upkeep problem, not an output problem, and one measured a cost of its change in the same 12 runs. Several of these pages record a mistake of mine along the way.

The last three all supported their change. The missing-record step carried over from the CRM hygiene skill to its prompt. And a second business case line brought back the hours total that the guardrail in the [prompt guardrail test](evaluations/business-case-prompt-guardrail-test.md) had suppressed, without bringing back the pound figure, first on the prompt and then on the skill.
